When Does a Listed Company Need a Registered Valuer?

The four SEBI triggers every company secretary should check before a preferential issue or scheme

By CA Rahul Nathani November 15, 2026 6 min read

"Do we need a Registered Valuer for this preferential issue?" is one of the most common questions a listed company's secretarial team faces, and the honest answer is "sometimes". For most preferential issues, SEBI's pricing formula does the work and no valuation report is required. But there are four situations where an independent Registered Valuer becomes mandatory, and missing one of them can hold up shareholder approval or the in-principle approval from the exchange. This note sets out all four.

The starting point: the Companies Act does not apply here

For an unlisted company, a preferential allotment under Section 62(1)(c) has to be priced on a Registered Valuer's report. Listed companies are treated differently. The Share Capital and Debentures Rules leave the pricing of a listed company's preferential issue to SEBI, so the question is answered entirely by Chapter V of the SEBI (ICDR) Regulations, 2018.

The default: frequently traded shares are priced on a formula

Shares are "frequently traded" if the traded turnover on any recognised exchange in the 240 trading days before the relevant date is at least 10% of the total shares of that class. For these companies, if the shares have been listed for 90 trading days or more, Regulation 164(1) sets the floor price as the higher of the 90 trading day and the 10 trading day volume weighted average price. No valuer is needed for that calculation.

One exception catches people out: the company's own Articles of Association. In 2021, SEBI directed PNB Housing Finance not to act on a preferential issue until it obtained the Registered Valuer's report its Articles called for, even though the price met the Regulation 164 formula. The Securities Appellate Tribunal split on the question and the company eventually dropped the issue, so the point was never finally settled. Check the Articles before assuming the formula is the end of it.

Trigger 1: the shares are not frequently traded (Regulation 165)

If the 10% turnover test is not met, there is no reliable market price to use. Regulation 165 then requires the price to be determined by an independent Registered Valuer, taking into account book value, comparable trading multiples and any other parameters customary for valuing shares of that kind of company. Thinly traded small caps and SME-platform companies fall into this more often than they expect, so the turnover test is worth running early.

Trigger 2: a large allotment or a change in control (Regulation 166A)

This is the trigger introduced in January 2022, and it applies even when the shares are frequently traded. A valuation report from an independent Registered Valuer is required where the preferential issue:

  • results in a change in control of the company, or
  • results in allotment of more than 5% of the post-issue fully diluted share capital to one allottee, or to allottees acting in concert.

The floor price then becomes the higher of the Regulation 164 formula price and the price determined by the valuer. Where control changes hands, the report also has to give guidance on control premium, computed over and above the formula price. In practice, this is where the valuer's work matters most, because a control premium is a judgement that the board, the exchange and minority shareholders will all read closely.

Trigger 3: shares issued for consideration other than cash (Regulation 163(3))

Since the January 2022 amendments, a preferential issue for consideration other than cash can only be a swap of shares, and the swap has to be supported by a valuation report from an independent Registered Valuer. The report is submitted to the stock exchanges where the issuer's shares are listed, and if an exchange is not satisfied with it, the exchange can have the valuation done by another valuer. The question here is not only what the listed company's shares are worth, but whether the shares it receives in the swap support the number of shares being issued.

Trigger 4: schemes of arrangement

Mergers, demergers and other schemes involving a listed entity run through SEBI's master circular on schemes of arrangement. The listed entity files a valuation report from a Registered Valuer with the stock exchange, and the audit committee considers it before recommending the scheme. A SEBI-registered merchant banker then gives a fairness opinion on that valuation, and the valuer and the merchant banker have to be independent of each other. Where an unlisted company is merging into a listed one, the listed entity also has to submit a Registered Valuer's report for the unlisted entity.

A quick checklist before the board meeting

  • Run the 240 trading day turnover test. If it fails, you need a valuer under Regulation 165.
  • Work out each allottee's post-issue fully diluted holding, including persons acting in concert. Above 5%, or any change in control, means Regulation 166A applies.
  • Check whether any part of the consideration is not cash. If so, it must be a share swap, and Regulation 163(3) requires a valuation report for it.
  • Read the Articles of Association for any valuation requirement of their own.
  • If it is a scheme, plan for the valuer's report and the merchant banker's fairness opinion together, as the second depends on the first.

Why the timing matters

The relevant date, the notice to shareholders and the exchange filing all run on fixed timelines. A valuation report that arrives after the notice has been drafted forces a rework, and a control premium that has not been thought through is the point most likely to draw a query. Bringing the valuer in when the deal terms are being settled, not after, avoids both.
