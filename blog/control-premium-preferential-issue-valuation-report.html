Control Premium in Preferential Issues: What the RV Report Must Cover

Regulation 166A of the SEBI ICDR Regulations, what it leaves to judgement, and what the board should expect from the valuer

By CA Rahul Nathani November 17, 2026 6 min read

When a listed company's preferential issue may result in a change in control, Regulation 166A of the SEBI ICDR Regulations asks for more than a price. The independent registered valuer's report must also give guidance on control premium. The regulation says that guidance is needed but not how to arrive at it. This note covers what the rule says, what it leaves to judgement, and what a report should contain so the board, the independent directors and shareholders can rely on it.

What Regulation 166A requires

SEBI inserted Regulation 166A in January 2022. For a preferential issue that may result in a change in control, or in an allotment of more than 5% of the post-issue fully diluted share capital to one allottee or to allottees acting in concert:

  • The issuer must obtain a valuation report from an independent registered valuer and consider it when fixing the price.
  • The floor price is the highest of three figures: the floor price under Regulation 164, the price in the valuer's report, and the price under the Articles of Association, where the Articles provide one.
  • If the issue is likely to result in a change in control, the report must also cover guidance on control premium. The control premium is computed over and above the floor price arrived at above.
  • The report must be published on the issuer's website, and the notice calling the general meeting must refer to it.
  • Under Regulation 166A(2), an issue that may result in a change in control can only be made on a reasoned recommendation from a committee of independent directors. The committee considers all aspects of the issue, including pricing, and the voting pattern of its meeting is disclosed in the general meeting notice. All the independent directors must attend that meeting.

What the regulation leaves open

The text asks for guidance on control premium. It does not prescribe a method, a range or a minimum percentage, and it does not by itself state that the premium must be added to the issue price. The decision on price rests with the board and shareholders, on the independent directors' recommendation. The valuer's job is to give them a reasoned, evidence-based view they can act on.

Because the premium is computed over and above the floor price, the starting point matters. The valuer should first be clear which of the three figures sets the floor, and why, before discussing any premium on top of it.

Why control is worth something

A control premium reflects what a controlling holder can do that a minority holder cannot: appoint the board, set strategy and dividend policy, restructure the business, and decide on capital raising or exit. Market prices on a stock exchange are prices for small, non-controlling trades. So a price based on market trading, such as the Regulation 164 floor, generally reflects a minority value. The control premium guidance addresses the gap between that minority value and what control is worth in the particular case.

What the control premium guidance should cover

  • The basis of the floor. Which figure sets the floor price under Regulation 166A, with the computation, so the premium is clearly added to the correct base.
  • Whether control actually changes. The shareholding before and after the issue, the board rights and other rights the allottee gets, and any existing promoter or shareholder agreements. The benefit of control depends on the rights that actually pass.
  • Sources of value from control. What the incoming controller can realistically change in this company: cost, capital structure, related party flows, synergies with its own business, or strategic direction. Where these can be quantified, the report should do so.
  • Market evidence. Premiums observed in comparable Indian control transactions, such as open offers and other change-of-control deals in the same or similar sectors. The report should set out the selection basis and the limits of the data.
  • The SAST context. If the allotment triggers an open offer under the SEBI Takeover Regulations, the pricing interacts with the open offer price. The report should note this so the board sees the complete picture.
  • A range, with reasoning. Control premium guidance is a judgement. A reasoned range, with the factors that place the answer within it, is more useful to the independent directors than a single unexplained figure.
  • Limitations and sources. What information was used, what was not available, and what was assumed, in line with the valuation standards the valuer follows.

Common gaps in practice

  • A report that gives a value but treats control premium in one line, or with a percentage taken from general literature with no link to the company.
  • A premium applied to a value that already reflects control, for example a value based on control transactions, which counts control twice.
  • No analysis of whether the allottee actually gets control, given its rights and the existing shareholding.
  • A report that is not written with publication in mind. The report goes on the issuer's website and is referred to in the meeting notice, so shareholders will read it.

Questions for the board and the independent directors

  • Is the valuer independent of the company, its promoters and the allottee?
  • Does the report clearly show which figure sets the floor price?
  • Is the control premium guidance supported by evidence specific to this company and this transaction?
  • Does the report address the open offer, if one is triggered?
  • Is the report clear enough to publish and to support a reasoned recommendation?
